# Argument and Flow Guide

Use this guide when drafting or substantively revising an IEEE paper whose argument feels fragmented, paragraphs do not build on one another, or sentence-level polishing has not improved readability. Keep the venue template, technical evidence rules, and engineering prose guidance in the main skill authoritative. Match the check to the requested scope: a paragraph edit needs its available neighbors; a section rewrite needs a section map; a full-paper rewrite needs manuscript-wide alignment.

## Establish the argument before drafting

From the available manuscript, notes, results, figures, and tables, answer four questions briefly for internal use: What exact difficulty motivates the work? What does this paper do about it? What evidence supports the answer? Under what conditions does the answer hold? Treat these answers as a working compass, not wording to repeat in the manuscript. If the evidence supports several possible stories, prefer the one the methods and primary result can actually test; surface consequential ambiguity instead of inventing a bridge.

For a substantial section or whole paper, map each main claim to the method or derivation that makes it testable, the result or proof that supports it, and the conclusion it permits. Check both directions: every advertised contribution should have a delivery point, and every major result should serve a stated question or contribution. Make the primary claim visible before secondary findings. Distinguish method capability from observed evidence and simulation findings from broader deployment claims.

## Plan the reader's path

Before prose, give each paragraph one reader job and a narrow defensible claim. Record, briefly and internally:

- **Function:** why this paragraph is here.
- **Claim:** the point the reader should take away.
- **Evidence:** the equation, design detail, result, figure, table, or verified citation that warrants it.
- **Development:** the reasoning that connects evidence to the claim, including necessary conditions.
- **Bridge:** the specific question, contrast, consequence, or qualification that makes the next paragraph necessary.

Arrange those jobs so prerequisites and definitions appear before use. Read the planned paragraph claims in sequence as a short outline. If two adjacent paragraphs perform the same job, merge or differentiate them. If the next paragraph requires an unstated premise, supply the premise or reorder the material. A transition phrase cannot repair a missing inference.

## Make prose carry the reasoning

Within each paragraph, let each sentence advance, support, explain, qualify, or close the main claim. Start from information the reader already has when that improves comprehension, then introduce the new object or result. Keep the grammatical subject stable enough to track the mechanism; repeat a technical term when it preserves identity instead of replacing it with a vague synonym. Name comparisons explicitly: what differs, under which condition, and by how much when the evidence provides a number.

Use a transition only when it names the real relation between ideas: contrast, extension, condition, cause, consequence, or qualification. Do not force a transition sentence at every paragraph end. A paragraph may end once its point is established, provided the next paragraph begins from a clear relation to it. Avoid sentences that simply announce a section or restate the previous sentence without adding a reason, observation, or implication.

## Review the actual draft

1. Read only the opening or claim sentence of each paragraph. Check whether they form a cumulative argument rather than a list of adjacent topics.
2. At each paragraph boundary, state what the previous paragraph established and what the next adds. Repair missing reasoning, order, or referents before changing connective words.
3. Read each paragraph with its immediate neighbors. Verify that its evidence warrants its claim and that its ending has not silently changed scope or causal strength.
4. For a whole manuscript, compare the Introduction's promises, Methods, primary figures or tables, Results, Abstract, and Conclusion. They should describe the same contribution at evidence-supported strength.
5. Read the final prose at normal speed. If meaning depends on rereading, shorten a noun chain, restore an explicit subject, move a condition closer to its claim, or split a sentence carrying multiple logical relations. Recheck technical meaning after the edit.

Keep the argument map and paragraph notes internal when the user asks only for manuscript text. For a diagnostic request, report the specific broken link and a concrete repair; do not describe weak flow merely as a style problem.
